HVAC airflow and ventilation calculations

HVAC design often starts with a room, a required air-change rate or a known airflow and then moves through related checks. CFM describes airflow, air changes per hour connects airflow with room volume, and duct velocity helps evaluate how that airflow moves through a duct. Duct sizing then turns the required airflow into a practical duct dimension, while fan CFM can be used as a quick airflow check.

These tools are useful for searches such as CFM calculator, airflow calculator, air changes calculator, duct size calculator, duct velocity calculator and fan CFM calculator. CalcForge keeps the tools linked so a user can move from one HVAC calculation to the next instead of relying on isolated answers.

How the HVAC calculator cluster fits together

CFM → Air Changes → Duct Size → Duct Velocity → Fan CFM is a practical workflow for many preliminary airflow questions. Start with the known quantity, calculate the missing airflow or room-change value, then check the duct dimensions and velocity. Thermal load and equipment selection are separate design steps and should not be inferred from airflow alone.

For real projects, verify room loads, outdoor-air requirements, pressure losses, filter and coil resistance, equipment fan curves, duct system design and the applicable local requirements. CalcForge results are preliminary calculation aids, not a substitute for a complete HVAC design.

Common HVAC calculation questions

How do I calculate CFM?

CFM can be calculated from room volume and air changes, or from other airflow relationships depending on the design problem. Use the CFM calculator with consistent dimensions and a clearly defined design basis.

How do I calculate air changes per hour?

Air changes per hour can be estimated from airflow and room volume using ACH = CFM × 60 ÷ room volume in cubic feet. The required ACH depends on the space and design criteria.

How do I size a duct from CFM?

Duct sizing normally considers required airflow together with an acceptable velocity or pressure-loss target. A single dimension from CFM alone is not a complete duct design.
