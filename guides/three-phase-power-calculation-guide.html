3 Phase Power Calculation: kW, kVA, Current and Power Factor

Three-phase systems are widely used for commercial, industrial and motor loads. Understanding the relationship between voltage, current, power factor and real power makes electrical sizing and troubleshooting much easier.

What is three-phase power?

A balanced three-phase system contains three AC phases separated by 120 electrical degrees. Compared with a single-phase system, three-phase distribution can transfer substantial power efficiently while keeping conductor currents manageable. Common system voltages include 208 V, 400 V and 480 V line-to-line configurations.

Three-phase real power formula

For a balanced three-phase load, real power can be expressed as P = √3 × V × I × PF, where P is watts, V is line-to-line voltage, I is line current and PF is power factor. Dividing watts by 1,000 gives kilowatts. This relationship is widely useful for estimating current from known load power.

What is power factor?

Power factor represents the relationship between real power and apparent power. A purely resistive load can have a power factor close to 1, while motors and inductive equipment can have lower values. Because current depends on power factor, using PF = 1 for every real-world load can underestimate current.

kW versus kVA

kW describes real power that performs useful work or produces heat, while kVA describes apparent power. For a balanced system, apparent power can be estimated from S = √3 × V × I. Power factor connects the two: kW = kVA × PF. Transformers and many distribution components are commonly rated in kVA.

Worked example

Consider a balanced 400 V three-phase load drawing 20 A at a power factor of 0.90. Real power is approximately √3 × 400 × 20 × 0.90 = 12,470 W, or about 12.47 kW. Apparent power is approximately √3 × 400 × 20 = 13.86 kVA. These values help establish the electrical scale of the load before conductor and protection selection.

Line voltage and phase voltage

In a common balanced wye system, line-to-line voltage and line-to-neutral phase voltage have a √3 relationship. This distinction matters because formulas must use the voltage type expected by the equation. Always identify whether a stated voltage is line-to-line or line-to-neutral before calculating current or power.

Three-phase power and wire sizing

Once load current is known, conductor sizing can be evaluated using ampacity and voltage drop. A three-phase power calculation does not automatically determine wire size or breaker size. Installation method, conductor material, temperature, continuous load requirements and applicable code provisions still need to be checked.

Common calculation mistakes

Common mistakes include using a single-phase formula on a three-phase load, confusing line voltage with phase voltage, ignoring power factor, mixing kW and kVA, and assuming a balanced-load equation applies unchanged to an unbalanced system. Identify the system configuration before calculating.

Professional design check

Three-phase calculations are excellent for estimating load current and power, but final electrical design should account for actual equipment data, harmonics where relevant, unbalanced loads, fault conditions, conductor ampacity, protection and applicable electrical requirements.
