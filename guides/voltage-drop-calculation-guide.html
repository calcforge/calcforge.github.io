Voltage Drop Calculation: Formula, Limits and Wire Sizing

Voltage drop is the reduction in circuit voltage caused by conductor impedance. Understanding voltage drop calculation helps you choose a practical wire size, evaluate long runs and reduce unwanted power loss.

What is voltage drop?

Every conductor has resistance, and AC circuits also have reactance. When current flows through a conductor, some voltage is consumed by the circuit conductors. The difference between the source voltage and the load voltage is voltage drop. Excessive voltage drop can cause poor motor performance, dim lighting, heating, nuisance problems and inefficient equipment operation.

Voltage drop formula

A simplified DC or resistive circuit relationship is Vd = I × R, where Vd is voltage drop in volts, I is current in amperes and R is conductor resistance in ohms. For practical wire sizing, resistance is obtained from conductor data and the complete circuit length is considered. Single-phase two-wire circuits normally account for the outgoing and return conductors, while three-phase calculations use the appropriate three-phase relationship.

How wire length affects voltage drop

Voltage drop increases as conductor length increases and as current increases. This is why a conductor that works well for a short branch circuit may produce unacceptable voltage loss on a long wire run. Conductor material, temperature, conductor size and installation conditions also affect resistance. Copper and aluminum do not have identical resistance, so the material must be included in a proper design calculation.

Voltage drop percentage

Voltage drop percentage can be calculated as voltage drop divided by source voltage, multiplied by 100. For example, a 3 V drop on a 120 V circuit is 2.5 percent. Designers commonly use project or code guidance around acceptable voltage drop, but the correct target depends on the installation, equipment and applicable requirements. Do not treat a generic percentage as a substitute for the governing electrical standard or manufacturer instructions.

Worked example

Suppose a 120 V load draws 15 A and the calculated circuit voltage drop is 4 V. The percentage drop is 4 ÷ 120 × 100 = 3.33 percent, leaving approximately 116 V at the load. If the design target is lower than this result, the next step is to evaluate a larger conductor, shorter route, different conductor material or other design change.

How voltage drop relates to wire sizing

Wire sizing should not be based on voltage drop alone. First consider allowable ampacity, conductor temperature rating, insulation, terminal ratings, installation method, ambient temperature, number of current-carrying conductors and applicable NEC or local requirements. After ampacity is satisfied, voltage drop can be checked to determine whether the selected conductor also provides acceptable load voltage.

AC, DC and three-phase considerations

DC and simple resistive AC circuits can often be estimated with resistance-based equations. Alternating-current circuits may require impedance, including reactance and power factor, especially for longer runs or larger conductors. Three-phase systems use a different voltage-drop relationship from single-phase circuits. Always use a calculation method appropriate to the circuit type and conductor arrangement.

Common voltage drop mistakes

Frequent mistakes include using one-way length when a two-conductor circuit requires the full circuit path, mixing metres with feet, confusing wire gauge with ampacity, ignoring conductor temperature, using the wrong phase configuration and assuming the calculator result is a code approval. Consistent units and verified conductor data are essential.

Final design check

A voltage drop calculation is one part of electrical design. Before installation, verify the final conductor selection, protection, installation method, equipment requirements and local electrical rules. For motors, sensitive electronics and long feeders, manufacturer requirements and project specifications can be especially important.
