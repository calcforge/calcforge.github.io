How to Choose the Right Electrical Wire Size

Choosing wire size is a combination of electrical load, allowable ampacity, voltage drop, conductor material and installation conditions. This guide explains the main factors behind practical AWG wire sizing.

What does wire size mean?

Electrical wire size describes the conductor cross-sectional area and, in North American systems, is commonly expressed using AWG or American Wire Gauge. A smaller AWG number generally represents a larger conductor. Larger conductors can carry more current and normally have lower resistance, which can also reduce voltage drop.

Start with the electrical load

Determine the expected current before selecting a conductor. The load may be given directly in amperes or calculated from power and voltage. Motors, heating equipment, lighting, continuous loads and electronic equipment can have different design requirements. The circuit breaker rating should not simply be copied as the conductor ampacity without checking the applicable rules.

Check ampacity first

Ampacity is the maximum current a conductor can carry under specified conditions without exceeding its temperature limitations. Proper ampacity selection considers conductor insulation, terminal temperature rating, ambient temperature, number of current-carrying conductors and installation method. NEC tables and applicable correction or adjustment factors should be used for actual installations.

Then check voltage drop

A conductor can meet ampacity and still create too much voltage drop on a long circuit. Voltage drop depends on current, conductor resistance, length, material and circuit configuration. For long branch circuits and feeders, evaluate voltage drop after the ampacity-based conductor selection.

Copper versus aluminum

Copper and aluminum conductors have different electrical resistance and physical properties. A conductor size that is suitable in copper may not have the same performance in aluminum. Termination requirements also differ, so the conductor material must be considered together with equipment ratings and manufacturer instructions.

Example wire sizing workflow

Imagine a 120 V branch circuit with a design current of 20 A and a long cable route. First determine the required ampacity from the applicable rules. Next select a conductor size that meets the ampacity conditions. Finally calculate voltage drop for the actual one-way route and circuit configuration. If voltage drop is excessive, evaluate a larger conductor rather than assuming the first ampacity-compliant size is always sufficient.

AWG and metric wire sizes

AWG is common in the United States and several other markets, while metric conductor cross-sectional area is commonly expressed in mm². AWG and mm² are not perfectly interchangeable labels, so use a recognized conversion or conductor table rather than treating them as identical sizes. Wire size and breaker size

The breaker protects the circuit under the governing electrical design rules, while the conductor must be selected for the load and installation conditions. A larger breaker does not automatically justify a smaller conductor. The relationship among load, conductor ampacity, overcurrent protection and equipment terminals must be evaluated together.

Common wire sizing mistakes

Common errors include choosing wire from a generic online chart without checking installation conditions, ignoring voltage drop, confusing AWG numbers, mixing metric and imperial units, overlooking continuous-load requirements and treating a calculator as a replacement for the electrical code. These shortcuts can produce unsafe or noncompliant designs.

Professional verification

Final conductor selection should be verified against the applicable NEC or local electrical code, manufacturer data, project specifications and site conditions. The goal is not merely to find a wire that carries the current, but to create a complete circuit that operates reliably and safely.
